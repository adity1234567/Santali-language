Abstract


This field-oriented study investigates the syntactic structure of the Santali language as spoken by the Santal community in Gopalpur, Rajshahi. While historically classified as a North Munda language within the Austroasiatic family, the Santali spoken in this region exhibits exclusive contact-induced and dialectal variations. Utilising a qualitative approach in which the primary data were collected from native speakers (N=12), this research evaluates the key syntactic operations, including word order, agreement, negation strategies, WH formation and other aspects of the Santali syntax. Major findings unveil a Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) head-final structure that remains resilient despite lexical borrowing from Bengali. Crucially, the study recognises an exclusive dialectal phonological fusion in the first-person negative marker [bo-iɲ], a feature not extensively documented in standard literature. Furthermore, the analysis initiates a syntactic hierarchy for the Zero Copula, which is permissible in attributive clauses but blocked in existential contexts. The study also archives the Genitive Case Collapse in third-person pronouns, where honorific distinctions are neutralised. These findings help to achieve a broader interpretation of Munda syntax and document the specific sociolinguistic reality of the Rajshahi Santal community.

Literature Review


Before conducting this study, we had gone through quite a few books and dissertations that acted as the backbone of our research work. Primarily, the book titled “Bangladesher Adibashi Bhasha” authored by Shourav Sikder (2020) provides a broad description of the phonology, morphology, and syntax of the Santali language. Another book that we used as our reference point is “The Munda Languages”, edited by Gregory D.S. Anderson (2008). The Chapter on Santali in Anderson (2008) is penned by Arun Ghosh. This chapter tries to capture the linguistic tendencies of the language meticulously. Two of the books written on the grammar of Santali that we consulted for our research are “Santali Primer” by F.T. Cole (1896) and “A Grammar of the Santhal Language” by L.O. Skrefsrud (1873). These two books are some of the earliest texts that strive to depict the grammar of the Santali language. Santali, one of the most widely spoken Austro-Asiatic (AA) languages from the North Munda branch, is an interesting case study that forces linguists to reconsider vital grammar rules. Unlike English or Hindi, where a word is generally born a Noun (N) or a Verb (V) and stays that way, Santali roots are flexible, meaning a single word can play different roles depending on the surrounding sentence structure. This flexibility is what we call fluid lexical categorisation.


1. The Core Puzzle: When a Noun Becomes a Verb
The central finding in Santali syntax is that the N−V distinction isn't set in stone in the dictionary; it's decided in the sentence structure itself. The process that makes this happen is called Verbalisation.


1.1. Syntactic Re-Categorisation
Research strongly suggests that a word (whether an N or an Adjective (A)) must first exist as its category before it can be 'verbalised'. This isn't a random, unpredictable process; it's a compositional syntactic mechanism that adds verbal features to a non-verbal core.
Denominal Verbalisation in Action: The most common example involves taking a noun, like raajaa ('king'), and turning it into a verb that means 'to become a king'.
The structure works by attaching the verbal complex directly to the noun: uni raajaa−en−a−e (He king−PST.MID−FIN−3SG) is perfectly grammatical Santali for 'He became a king.'


1.2. Why It Isn't Just a Hidden Word
A critical part of the academic argument involves proving that these non-verbal items are truly becoming verbs, not just sitting next to a silent word like 'become' (a Null Copula). Diagnostics like NP Scrambling are used to show that the Santali sentence behaves differently from languages that use a hidden copula, thereby confirming the direct verbalisation of the N or A root in the syntax.


2. Fieldwork Insights: Predication and Simplicity
Our fieldwork task perfectly illustrates the practical reality of this flexible system, especially in basic sentence types and word classes.


2.1. The Efficient Copula
In simple identity or class membership sentences, Santali shows remarkable efficiency. The copula verb kan ('to be') is frequently noted as 'optional'. This pliability enables simple nominal predication, in which a sentence like 'I am a man' can be rendered as simply in hɔr ('I man'), relying on context and the lack of a verbal marker to indicate the noun's predicative role.


2.2. Simplicity in Agreement
Possessive structures (e.g. using ina? 'my' or unia? 'his/her') show no distinction based on the age or gender of the possessor. This lack of gender-based agreement is a distinct simplification compared to Indo-Aryan languages, which often co-exist with Santali. Santali’s fluid lexical system and its syntax-driven verbalisation process make it a standout language for modern linguistic research. By systematically altering non-verbal roots into predicates, Santali illustrates a grammatical scheme that prioritises the sentence structure over the lexicon in assigning basic word function.


The article “Santals and Their Language: An Anthropological Study” (2021) by Dr Nizamuddin Ahmed and the introduction to "Advances in Munda Linguistics”, edited by Shailendra Mohan, are two significant works on Santali language studies that we are reviewing here. The goal is to comprehend how these work to advance our understanding of the Santali language, especially about Santali syntax as a field of study. Also, we will point out the gaps.


 An analysis of "Santals and their Language: An Anthropological Study" by Dr Nizamuddin Ahmed (2021) primary attention in contrast to a purely linguistic approach, DDrAhmed's article takes an anthropological and socio-cultural approach to studying the Santali language. The study mainly concentrates on two : 
* The history and evolution of the Ol Chiki script. 
* The contemporary educational and social issues facing the Santali language are the study's two primary focuses. 


The article gives important background information about the Santal community and their linguistic identity, but it doesn't go into the details about the language's syntax or grammatical structure. Dr Nizamuddin Ahmed tries to focus on the social and cultural aspects of the Santali language –


1. Language and community identity: According to the 2001 Census, there are over 6.5 million Santal speakers, making them the third largest tribal group in India after the Bhil and Gond tribes. Santali is now the 14th most spoken language of the 22 languages listed in the Indian Constitution. The Santals refer to themselves as "Hor," which means "Man," and their language as "Hor-Ror," where "Ror" means speech or language. This shows that the Santal people’s ethnic and linguistic identity is intertwined. 


2. Classification of language families: According to Schmidt's 1906 classification, this article verifies that Santali is a member of the Munda group of languages, which is categorised under the Austro-Asiatic language family. This data is important because it places Santali in a larger linguistic framework and helps in understanding how it relates to other languages.


3. The history of how writing systems have changed over time: Ahmed (2021) emphasises that Santali was written before Ol Chiki was created. Santali was only spoken until the 19th century. Before the 1860s, Christian missionaries started writing Santali using Bengali and Roman scripts. Other writers used the Devanagari and Oriya scripts. This caused problems because different areas used different scripts, making it hard to keep the written language the same.
 
4. The invention of the Ol Chiki script: Pandit Raghunath Murmu invented the Ol Chiki script in 1925 and described its linguistic construction. The Ol Chiki script consists of six vowels (Raha Arang) and twenty-four consonants (Kecheth Arang). It also has letters that change the tone, nasalisation, length, and deglottalization of words. The article also talks about the myths and stories that led to the script's creation. It shows how many of the characters came from nature, the physical world, and the way people normally stand 


5. The educational and social setting: The 92nd Constitutional Amendment made Santali a scheduled language in 2003. Ol Chiki became the official script for Santali in West Bengal in 1979, and Santali-medium schools have been set up in different districts since 2008–2009.


6 . Problems with the Santali language: The article talks about a few important problems. Such as the lack of well-trained teachers, the poor infrastructure for teaching Santali, the few job opportunities for Santali learners, and also the students have to switch to other languages in secondary school because Santali is not taught as a medium of instruction after primary school.


Limitations of this article are : 


No grammatical discussion 
This article solely focuses on the anthropological and social aspects of the Santal language. How the Santal language became a constitutional language, its social respect and status are only discussed. 


Focus on the script 
Although a script is important for any language and its preservation, it does not show how the language is constructed.


No example of the then Santali language 
The written form of any language changes over time, so if there were any examples, then we could have easily compared the new written form and the then written form and could evaluate how much it has changed.


“Advances in Munda Linguistics” (2021) by Shailendra Mohan and Masato Kobayashi discusses the recent studies on Munda languages, including Santali, in its introduction. This introduction is written by an Austroasiatic linguistics expert, Shailendra Mohan, who provides important background information for understanding Santali's place in the Munda language family and the characteristics that connect these languages. 


The introduction part of this book identifies some important points :


1. Munda languages in the South Asian setting
Munda languages play a significant but least studied role in South Asian linguistics. These languages are different from the predominant Indo-Aryan and Dravidian language families in India because they are members of the Austroasiatic family. Munda languages are still among the least documented in the Indian subcontinent, despite being spoken by almost ten million people.


2. Comparative and historical views 
The book discusses the historical evolution of various Munda languages, such as Santali, and the reconstruction of proto-Munda. The proto-Munda speakers most likely came from Indo-China to the border of Odisha between 4000 and 3500 years ago. It is important to have an idea about this historical context to understand how Santali evolved into its present syntactic characteristics. 


3. Typological features
Typological characteristics of Munda languages are covered in the introduction.   Santali and Sora are thought to be less restructured than other Munda languages, which is important for syntax research. This indicates that they have had less contact induced by the Indo-Aryan and the Dravidian languages and have gained more of the original Munda linguistic characteristics. 


4. Contact-Induced change and word order
The fact that Santali switched from SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) to SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order due to contact with Indo-Aryan languages is one of the most important contributions to syntax research. Also, the introduction points out that Santali automatically uses a topic-prominent clause structure. This knowledge is important to comprehending Santali syntax and its historical development. 


5. Conservatism in phonology Santali has a larger vowel system than related languages and is phonologically conservative within the Munda branch. Although this is phonological information, it indicates that Santali might also maintain other conservative elements that might be important for Santali syntax.


6. Comparative structure 
The introduction offers a framework for understanding what distinguishes Santali syntax from that of its sister languages by placing it within the larger Munda family. Because it makes it easier to identify which traits are inherited from proto-Munda and which evolved independently, this comparative viewpoint is useful for syntax research.


Instead of offering a thorough analysis, it provides an introduction to an edited volume. There is insufficient elaboration on certain syntactic constructions, rules, and patterns. Wide-ranging attention to various languages, Santali-specific syntactic details are naturally limited due to the volume's coverage of several Munda languages. As it focuses on all the Munda languages, there are no specific details about any one language. The introduction focuses a lot on comparative factors and historical reconstruction. These are important, but they don't give enough space to discuss synchronic syntactic description, or how contemporary Santali syntax actually functions.
 
The analysis of these two works reveals several important research gaps regarding Santali syntax: 


1. Fundamental Clause Structure The fundamental Santali clause structure is not fully described in either work. Santali has SOV word order and is topic-prominent, as we know from the Munda linguistic introduction, but there are still a lot of unanswered questions.


2. Sentence Structure Complexity
The formation of complex sentences in Santali is not covered in either work.
It does not involve where relative clauses appear in relation to the noun they modify, and how they are formed. It also does not include what kinds of complement clauses there are, the structure of adverbial clauses and the complex predicate type clause.


3. Structure of noun phrases
The structure of Santali noun phrases is not explained in either work. The noun phrase's elemental order (noun, adjective, demonstrative, numeral, possessor), how possessive relationships are labelled, the case-marking system, and what kinds of determiners there are, are not discussed. 


4. Forming and negation 
 There is no discussion of these fundamental syntactic processes. How do the Santals formulate a yes-no question? Which question words are present in the clause, and how do they use the negation marker?


5. Syntactic change induced by contact 
Santali changed from SVO to SOV due to Indo-Aryan contact, according to the Munda linguistics introduction, but this deserves further research. 


3. What we did in our field work to fill these gaps
* We worked with the basic sentence structure of the Santali language. Santali follows the SOV structure. 
* We worked with the negation markers and explored variations among negation particles.
* We worked with how the copula form works in the sentence structure. There are many zero copulas like the Bangla language structure. 
* How is it a contact-induced language and is influenced by other languages like Bangla, Sadri, and Hindi
* We identified the interpersonal relationship between free pronouns and how honorifics work in Santali.
* We recognised the Wh-markers and their positions within sentences
* Our data included unique findings regarding honorifics, person-induced negations and dialectal variations, dual vs plural forms in natural speech and everyday contexts, zero copula in some aspects and so on.
Finally, our research objective and findings focused on the data collected from the site and the findings were based on the less documented yet intriguing syntactic aspects.


































































Research Methodology 


1. Research Design


The Santali language's syntax was investigated in this study through a qualitative research design. We ratified the qualitative method since it concentrates on comprehending language in depth rather than gathering data or statistics. The qualitative research design made it effortless for us to distinguish patterns that might not be revealed in a more constructed or numerical study and to examine how people actually used the language in everyday contexts. We also examined how sentences were constructed, how words were arranged, and how syntax conveyed meaning by employing a qualitative approach. Some statistical analyses were also employed to maintain the data efficiently and to understand the data ratio. Uninterrupted communication with native Santali speakers was another aspect of this design that allowed for the collection of comprehensive linguistic data. Intending to capture significant linguistic details that might otherwise be overlooked in a quantitative study, it is obligatory to observe and analyse real speech. Through the natural and adaptable combination of observation, interaction, and linguistic analysis, our qualitative research design enabled us to acquire a thorough understanding of Santali syntax. Moreover, the statistical analysis gave us clear insights into the participants and their backgrounds.

4. Data Collection Process


4.1 Session Plan and Execution


Total number of sessions: We conducted a total of 10 sessions, including filler sessions for cross-checking data and filling gaps from previous sessions. 
Duration of each session: Among these 10 sessions, we had 6 sessions of approximately 45-60 minutes each and 4 sessions of approximately 2-2.5 hours each. 
Frequency: Our total data collection period was 2 days. The first day, we conducted 6 small sessions. On the second day, we conducted 2 longer, in-depth parallel sessions along with our 2 filler sessions. 
  





4.2 Audio Recording 


Recording equipment: We recorded the data on our smartphone with the recording app “Easy Voice”.


Audio format:  We recorded the data in WAV format.


Audio duration limit: Total recording time 346 minutes.


Total audio data collected: We collected approximately 5.8 hours of audio data across all sessions.


Storage and backup: Audio files were stored on personal smartphones and a specific Google Drive with regular backups to ensure data security.


4.3 Primary Data


Audio recordings: From conversations and interviews, we gathered natural speech data. 


Transcriptions: All Santali audio recordings have been IPA transcribed. 


Field notes: Observational notes made during sessions. 


Researcher’s journal: Researchers' thoughts on linguistic trends, difficulties, and revelations during fieldwork.


4.4 Secondary Data


Linguistic examples: Sentences that were selected for syntactic analysis. 


Explanations from participants: commentary on grammar rules from a meta-linguistic perspective. 


Contextual details: Information about the topics and settings of conversations.

5.3 Analysis Procedure


We analysed the data following these steps:
* Familiarisation: Reading transcripts and listening to audio recordings repeatedly. 
* Coding: Recognising and labelling patterns and syntactic structures.

* Comparison: Verifying results from various sessions and speakers. 
* Interpretation: Making inferences about Santali syntax from patterns found.
6. Analytical Framework
The analytical framework was guided by descriptive linguistics. Some major theories that we followed during the analysis are-


1. The Boasian Trilogy: The three crucial elements of a comprehensive language description and documentation project, as promoted by Franz Boas and his students, are referred to as the "Boasian trilogy" in descriptive linguistics and anthropology. The three elements are: 


* A collection of texts: This entails compiling lengthy instances of the language in use, such as stories, customs, or casual conversations, frequently with free and interlinear translations. These texts serve as a record of the verbal art and communication patterns of the culture and supply the primary data (raw data) for analysis. 


* A grammar: This part describes the rules and organisation of the language, including its phonological system, morphological word structure, and syntactic sentence construction. 


* A dictionary: This offers a lexicon of the language, describing word meanings, forms, and usage.


Instead of viewing this trilogy through the prism of a single, universal evolutionary path, Boas saw it as an anthropological endeavour as well as a linguistic one, crucial for comprehending a culture on its own terms (cultural relativism) and as an integrated whole (holism). The practice known as "salvage ethnography" aimed to provide a thorough record of a people's mental and cultural life, especially for endangered indigenous languages in America.


2. Dell Hymes ‘Ethnography of speaking’: Moving beyond abstract grammar to study communication as a social practice, Dell Hymes' "ethnography of speaking" (later "ethnography of communication") is a framework for comprehending how language is used in its cultural and social context. By examining the setting, participants, ends (goals), act sequence, key (tone), instrumentalities, norms, and genre, it employs the SPEAKING model to analyse communication. The method highlights that knowledge of these cultural and social aspects is just as important for effective communication as language proficiency.


The SPEAKING model-


An organised method for examining communication events is offered by the acronym SPEAKING: 


S-Setting and Scene: The communication's psychological and physical context. 
P-Participants: The people who attend and their roles. 
E-Ends: The communication's shared and individual objectives. 
A-Act Sequence: The speech acts' order and the types of utterances they contain. 
K-Key: The communication's tone or atmosphere, such as formal, lighthearted, or serious. 
I-Instrumentalities: The means of communication, such as a written letter or a phone call. 
N-Norms: The guidelines that control language usage in a speech community. 
G-Genre: The kind of communication, like a news report, a joke, or a sermon.

Informed consent: Participants gave their informed consent after being fully informed about their rights and the purpose of the study. 
Confidentiality: Pseudonyms were used to protect participant identities. Under no circumstances will their personal information be revealed or published.

1. Interpersonal Pronoun Systems
1.1 Pronominal Structure: Consistent Genitive Case Collapse
Our data represents a clear, uniform demonstration of how the pronominal genitive case collapses distinctions maintained in neighbouring Indo-Aryan languages (like Bangla).
unia-ʔ used for Bangla translations corresponding to: 
tar (2SG/3SG informal possessive) 
or (3SG familiar/intimate possessive) 
onar (3PL/3SG honorific possessive) 
Unique Feature:
Extreme Generality of the Third-Person Genitive. The single form uniaʔ (3SG/PL-GEN) is a fixed point in the pronominal system, unifying multiple person/status distinctions that are robustly maintained in the source language (Bangla).
Our fieldwork clearly documents the single form uniaʔ neutralising the honorific/familiar distinction (tar vs. Onar), a crucial point of divergence from Indo-Aryan contact languages that may not be available in general academic corpora, despite the literature’s general observation of a “lack of gender/age agreement.”
1.2 Morphological Persistence: Pronoun Dual Number
əliɲ (1st Person Inclusive Dual Pronoun, 'We two') 
ɑlæ (1st Person Inclusive Plural  Pronoun, 'We')
Unique Feature:
Explicit representation of the Dual and Plural distinction on 1st person Inclusive Pronouns in an immediate and analysed sentence structure. The Munda family is known to have three numbers (singular, dual, and plural), as evidenced by nouns like (seta-kin), which means “(two dogs).” However, our data shows that this morphological complexity persists in the usage of the personal pronouns (əliɲ ‘we-DUAL’ vs. ɑlæ ‘we-PLURAL’) in active sentences. Despite the simplifying effects of language contact, this phenomenon exemplifies Santali's morphological retention from its Austrasiatic legacy. ( Data from recording -Mayabi Murmu, Gorethi Hembrom(25-10)12:40-13:20)

 Specific forms for temporal and spatial questions that are either missing or not as well-detailed in the literature currently in publication are documented by our field research:
● Temporal: For "when," we discovered [t̪inɟokʰoi / t̪inɟukʰuem], which exhibits phonological variation not represented in the simple [tis] form used in existing literature. This implies that temporal questioning varies depending on the dialect or context.

2.3 Qualitative and Manner Interrogatives
   Several manner and quality interrogatives that go beyond the existing literature were found by our research:
●    The form [tʃikat̪e] (how/in what manner) differs from [ceka]/[cikə] as described in our literature review. Our reviewed literature did not include the qualitative interrogative [tʃet̪ilka] (what kind/what type). According to these results, Santali has a more comprehensive system for evaluating manners and quality than has been previously shown.
2.4 Quantitative Inquiries
      While [tinak'] (how much) is mentioned in the literature, our field data shows:
●  For counting discrete objects, [t̪inagan] (how many) appears to have a more general function than [t̪inoʔ] (how much/how many). A more complex quantification system in common Santali use is indicated by the differentiation between count and mass interrogatives. 
2.5 Interrogatives Based on Demonstrations 
 The frequency of forms starting with [oko-] or [oka-], such as [okoj]/[okoi] (who) and [okoiren] (whose/whom in plural), is a noteworthy discovery from our field research. [okoi](whose) [ukataʔ] (which one) these forms, which display morphological patterns where interrogative pronouns may derive from or share roots with demonstrative pronouns, assume a potential demonstrative origin for some interrogatives. Although this relationship between demonstratives and interrogatives is familiar across languages, it has not been thoroughly examined in the Santali literature that is currently obtainable.
2.6 Dialectal Forms and Phonological Variation:
●      Significant phonological variation is revealed by our field data: "When": [t̪inɟokʰoi] versus [t̪inɟukʰuem] "Where": [okat̪em] vs. [ukare] vs. [okaren]"Who": [Okoi] vs. [okoj kanaj]. Differences in dialect, register, or contextual usage patterns that are not encompassed in standardised grammatical descriptions could be the cause of this variation. The fact that there are several acceptable constructions for the same interrogative function indicates that Santali speakers are flexible in their question word selection, which may be influenced by regional variation, formality, or emphasis.
2.7 Positional Variation In Interrogatives
There is positional flexibility in the Santali language interrogations. According to our collected data, Santali interrogatives are not restricted to a single syntactic position. Rather, their arrangement is ambiguous and seems to be determined by the particular interrogative word and the object of the question. In our data, we have found sentence-initial, sentence-medial, and sentence-final positions of interrogatives in Santali. A system of fixed positions for individual lexemes rather than a single fixed position for all interrogatives is suggested by the data, which indicates that different interrogative words have a strong tendency to appear in specific positions. 
2.7.1 Initial Interrogatives in Sentences
 Interrogatives are frequently found at the start of sentences. This is typical for general "what" questions and questions concerning the subject, reason, and time.
 [okoi] (Who/Whose): Functions as the subject or a possessive modifier.
 Example: "[okoi heʔ i-na] " (Who came?)
 Example: "[okoi oɾa kan-a] " (Whose house is this?)
 [okaɾen] (Where): Questions location.
 Example: "[tʃe at̪u okaɾen kan-a-m] " (Where is your village?)
 [oka-t̪em] (Where - motion): Questions direction.
 Example: "[oka-t̪em tʃalaʔ kan-a] " (Where are you going?)
[amtʃe] (How): Question manner.
 Example: "[amtʃe likam kami-a] " (How is this to be done?)
 [t̪in ɟukʰuem] (When): Questions time.
 Example: "[t̪in ɟukʰuem tʃala] " (When will you return/go?)ʼ
 [ tʃe] (What): Often appears sentence-initially when questioning the object or nature of something.
 Example: "[tʃe t̪epe oɽae kid̪a] " (What is this house made of?)
 Example: "[tʃepe rohoi e-a ape-d̪o]" (What crops do you cultivate?)
2.7.2 Medial Interrogatives in Sentences 
The middle of the sentence is where other interrogatives are always found. This frequently happens when the interrogative is effectively incorporated into the sentence's main argument structure by questioning a possessor, a quantity, or a pronoun. Copula verbs often follow interrogatives in this position.
 [oko iɾen] (Whose): Appears before the copula verb.

 2.7.3 Final Interrogatives in Sentences
The sentence ends with a few interrogatives. This is especially evident in certain constructions, such as [tʃe] and for the question particle [kina]
[kina] (Question Particle): Functions as a yes/no question marker and is strictly sentence-final.
 Example : " [apeɾen-d̪o kada mina kina]?" (Do you have oxen?)
[tʃe] (What): Can also appear in sentence-final position, typically in questions about a name or identity.
Example: " [amaʔ not̪um tʃe]" (What is your name?)
 Example: " [am-pen gid̪ɾo not̪um d̪o tʃe]?" (What is your son's name?)
Throughout the syntactic structure, Santali interrogatives exhibit positional flexibility. The placement is lexically specified; that is, the position of the particular interrogative word—whether its grammatical role determines initial, medial, or final—. One important characteristic of Santali syntax that sets it apart from purely wh-fronting languages is its flexibility. Interrogatives can and do move across sentences, as the data demonstrates convincingly.

7. Copula


A copula refers to a grammatical component that connects the subject of a sentence to a predicate expressing identity, classification or a state. It can carry tense, aspect, mood, or agreement features in many languages. Copulas are central to comprehending non-verbal predicates.


7.1 Interrogative Copula Dropping


In Santali, the copula/ auxiliary can be dropped in certain pragmatic circumstances, including irritation and emphasis. Antecedent literatures mention tone, except for the systematic copula dropping patterns we documented. These are-


* ragat̪o hɔjena - "Did you do it?" (irritated tone)
* baŋ kɔ milid̪a nɔjakɔ binit̪a - "I told you to do this work, did you?"




7.2 Multiple Copular Strategies 


Our data shows that Santali uses many different ways to express “being”. We have overt copular forms like “mina”(present) and tahena / tehekana (past), also zero copula (ø.COP)
Where nothing appears overtly.

7.4 The Hierarchy of Copula Dropping


 While Santali has an overt copula (kan), our data recommends that it is not a prerequisite phenomenon in all contexts. We remarked a syntactic split based on the type of predication:


Existential/Locative Clauses
The copula or existential marker (mina) is mandatory.
	"uni orak-re mina-e-a"
 (He is in the house).


	Attributive/Adjectival Clauses
 The copula is optional and frequently dropped (Zero Copula).
	"uni hor [Ø] osul"
 (That man [is] tall)
	

This proves that Santali treats "existence" (being somewhere) in a different way than "attribution" (being something). In the data we assembled from the site, the Zero Copula strategies were almost exclusively found in attributive sentences, aligning with typological and structural patterns seen in other Austroasiatic languages.  The existential copula in our data does not align under the Zero Copula strategy, which is not documented in the previous literature, like Ghosh (2008).

7.5 Person and number sensitivity 


Copula changes based on who or what we are talking about. There is “minaʔpɟa” for singular and “minaʔqua” for plural, which suggests that the copula agrees with the subject.


7.6 Features that Differ from Existing Literature
7.6.1 Zero copula is a dominant feature
Overt copula verbs are often emphasised in standard descriptions of Santali, but our data indicates that zero copula appears frequently, mainly with locative predicates (ale at̪u gadaʔ ariræ), quality predicates(uni hɔrd̪ɔ ædi osul).
7.6.2 Variation based on Context 
Certain pragmatic or discourse factors that aren’t always covered in grammatical description may influence the decision between overt copula and zero copula. For example, a zero copula appears to be more preferred in temporal or locative contexts rather than existential ones.
7.6.3 The alternation between “tahena”, “tahekana” and “tehekana”
There are two past copular forms that we found. The majority of literature refers to tahe(n) as the standard past copula. In our collected data, “tahena” is used as the present form, whereas the “tehekana” and  “tahekana” variants are used as the past form. This indicates either a dialectal or a grammatical distinction which needs to be studied further. 
7.6.4 Combining auxiliary construction
The copula that follows an auxiliary verb (laŋga-gin) in “holadɔ ædi laŋga-gin tehekana” (yesterday I was very tired), illustrates how copulas interact with complex predicates, something that isnʼt always covered in description.
7.6.5 Patterns of question formation 
The number-marked question strategy of (minaʔpɟa vs minaʔqua) appears to be more systematic than is usually described.
7.6.6 Real speech patterns
We collected actual usage patterns rather than idealised grammatical examples. This actually demonstrates how copulas are actually used in speech 


8 . Information Structure: The Role of the -d̪o Marker


Our fieldwork data reveal that the suffix -d̪o is among the most frequent morphemes in Santali discourse.


8.1 Syntactic Elucidation: Topic vs. Focus
In linguistics, a sentence is often separated into two parts:


Topic (Theme): The "old" information; what the sentence is about.


Focus (Rheme): The "new" information; what is being said about the topic.


The marker -do attaches to the Topic of the sentence to indicate that this constituent is the "given" information.


Example from Data: uni-d̪ɔ         ba-e                      kami      kan-a 
                                 3SG-TOP   NEGː3SGːSUBJ  Work      COP-FIN
Here, uni (He) is the entity already known to the listener. The marker -d̪o flags him as the "Topic."The rest of the sentence (ba-e kami...) anticipates the new information (Focus).


8.2 Support from Literature


This finding aligns with Anderson's classification of Munda languages as having a "topic-prominent clause structure". Unlike English, where the Subject is defined by its position (start of the sentence), Santali uses morphology (-d̪o) to explicitly mark the topic, regardless of where it occurs in the sentence structure (though it is predominantly identified in the initial position).


8.3 Placement in the Clause


The data we collected implies that -do attach mostly to:


Pronominal Subjects: uni-d̪o (He-TOP), iɲ-d̪o (I-TOP), am-d̪o (You-TOP).
Demonstratives: nɔwa-d̪ɔ (This-TOP).
Noun Phrases: uni koɽa-d̪o (That boy-TOP).


8.4 Cognitive Function: The "Anchor"


Cognitively, -d̪o functions as an "anchor" for the listener. It tells the listener, "We are having a conversation about this particular person or object; now here is the news about them." If the -d̪o marker were withdrawn, the sentence might still be grammatical in some contexts, but it would overlook its discursive coherence, conceivably turning the subject into the "Focus" (new information) rather than the "Topic."

Case-Study: Santali Syntax


The Santals are one of Bangladesh's largest indigenous communities. According to the Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics (BBS) 2022 Census, the Santal population in the country is 129,049 people. Most of them live in the northern parts of the country, in the districts Rajshahi, Naogaon, Chapai Nawabganj, Dinajpur and Rangpur, etc.


Santali is the native language of the Santals. It belongs to the North Munda group of the Austro-Asiatic language family. In 1959, linguist Pinnow proposed a classification of the Austroasiatic language family. He divided the Austroasiatic language family into two central branches ( the Western group, the Eastern group). The Western  group was further divided into two( West  Northwest, Northwest(Munda))


Later, Pinnow classified Munda into four subgroups (Eastern, Western, Central and Southern). He included the Santali language(the largest Munda language) in the eastern subgroup.
  

 Figure 04: Classification of the Munda language according to Pinnow(1959).


In 1969, Zide classified Munda languages, which are different from Pinnow's(1959) classification. Zide combined Pinnow's Eastern and Western subgroups of Munda languages as North Munda, and he combined Central and Southern subgroups as South Munda. Zide’s classification is widely accepted and considered as “Traditional Classification of the Munda Languages”.
  

  Figure 05: Classification of the Munda language according to Zide(1969).


In 1975, Sudhuibhushan Bhattacharya proposed that Munda languages are ten in number and they are classified into four subbranches (Northwestern Munda, Northeastern Munda, Central Munda and Southern Munda). He included the Santali language in the Northeastern Munda group. 
  



   Figure 06: Classification of Munda languages by Shudhibhushan Bhattacharya (1975).


Anderson proposed the Munda classification in which North Munda is divided into two daughter groups, namely “Korku” and “Kherwarian”. The Santali language belongs to the Kherwarian branch.
  

   Figure 07: The Munda Languages by Anderson (2001).


Syntax is the study of the interrelationships between components of sentence structure and of the rules governing the arrangements of sentences in sequences. The typical arrangement of words in a phrase, clause, or sentence is referred to as word order. The natural word order of the Santali language is SOV (Subject-Object-Verb), like Bangla. The shared word order minimises overt conflict in sentence configuration, which allows for more subtle, internal structural convergence, particularly in complex phrase structures. The Santali-speaking communities of the northern part of Bangladesh (including Rajshahi, Naogaon, and Rangpur) are at a stage of intense linguistic pressure because the Santal community and the majority Bangla community are highly interconnected.


In these areas, Santals are usually bilingual, sometimes trilingual. There, Bangla serves as the dominant language of administration, education, and mainstream public interaction. The Santals use the Roman script for their religious scriptures. The validity of the Santal language is facing generational decline. Rather than Santali, the younger generations use Bangla for speaking, reading, writing, and everyday communication. They consider the Santali structures as an "obstacle" regarding marginalisation and social exclusion for using a non-dominant language.
The Santali language spoken in the Bangladeshi plain areas is undergoing a critical process of reducing the range of styles, genres, and complex grammatical features among the younger generation because of the limited input. Though globally the language is not classified as endangered, code-switching (the altering use of two languages within the same interaction) is common in Santali speakers. Besides, there is extensive borrowing of forms from one language into the matrix language (Santali).




Ethnography
The Santali Language, spoken by the Santal community, is one of the largest and oldest tribal languages in Bangladesh, with its own unique culture and artefacts. The ethnographic fieldwork was administered to identify Santali syntax, particularly the interpersonal relationship between free pronouns, the position of negation, copular forms, tag questions, and WH-formation. Moreover, we aimed to identify the word order in Santali to understand the patterns of sentences in naturally occurring Santali speech. The fieldwork was conducted in the Gopalpur area of Rajshahi, where 30 Santal families were residing.
Comprehending the Santali syntax is not only valuable for linguistic documentation but also crucial for preserving the endangered language and supporting pedagogy for indigenous communities. As Team Clause-trophobic, we addressed the linguistic diversity and the cultural diversity in the Santali community, acknowledging the existence of a language that is indispensable for identity and community life.
Methodology
Field Site: Our research was conducted in Gopalpur, a small area in Rajshahi, where 30 Santal families dwelled, maintaining their native language and ancestral cultures among themselves despite being surrounded by Bengali-dominated areas. The people were primarily engaged in agriculture, brick kiln work and some other forms of manual labour.
Duration: Our fieldwork was conducted in two days in October, mainly from 24th October to 25th October, 2025. We took our sessions from morning till noon. 
Methods Employed: 
* Participant observation: We observed natural conversations in homes and community spaces
* Semi-structured interviews: Conducted in a mix of Santali and Bengali with bilingual community members
* Audio recordings: With informed consent, we recorded videos of  conversational speech for later transcription and analysis
* Field notes: Maintained detailed sessions documenting linguistic observations, contextual information and environmental settings.
Access and Rapport: Our initial contact was made by a Pahariya teenager who had relatives in the Santal community. She guided us to the families and asked permission to continue the interview.

The historical foundations of the community in Gopalpur are really deep, with older residents confirming that their presence in this region predates any living memory. While talking to an elderly woman from the community, we found out that she has been living in this region since birth, and so have their ancestors, which proves a long connection to this place. However, their past is characterised by various challenges along with dislocation. They had to endure long-standing marginalisation. They were the first community to establish habitation in this particular area. Later, many other communities also started to come and live nearby, but they were hostile to the Santal community. The forcible seizure of crops by neighbouring muslim communities is one example of such hostility. The Santals, being a very small community in this region, were helpless against the dominant group. A significant event in their recent history was their escape to India during the 1971 Liberation War to secure their safety. During the Liberation War, many people of minority communities were tortured and killed. So, in fear for their life, the Santal community of Gopalpur village fled to India. But once Bangladesh became an independent country, they all returned to reclaim their village and have been living here since.


3. Sociolinguistic Profile: A Community in Transition
The sociolinguistic dynamics in Gopalpur have been undergoing swift changes in recent times, which present a considerable threat to the Santali language.


3.1 Multilingualism: 
Although Santali is the cultural mother tongue, the use of multiple languages is essential in the Santal community. Every member of the community has to rely on Bangla for everyday external communication and education, while English is used in both academic and religious settings. They lack the opportunity to get an education in their mother tongue, not even a primary education. So they must learn Bangla, and they can only use Santali in their own household. So naturally, they are focusing more on Bangla than Santali, and their fluency in Santali is decreasing with each generation.


3.2 Language Shift:
 A conspicuous alteration in language across generations is visible. While the older generation is still proficient in Santali, the younger generation shows a significant increase in inclination towards Bangla and a decrease in proficiency in their traditional language. We think the reason behind this phenomenon is the recent increase in literacy. The older generation is mostly illiterate. They didn’t have to go through formal education, and as a result, they only had to learn Bangla for external communication. So they have a better grasp of their own language. But the situation changes for the younger generation. With time, people became more aware of the importance of education and started sending their children to school more. So the younger generation had to learn Bangla and English both formally and again in any educational institution. They cannot use their mother tongue, so they lose grasp of their own language and prefer Bangla for their daily communication. This situation is exacerbated by the lack of formal mother-tongue education.


3.3 Literacy and Script: 
The state of Santali literacy is very critical. The community possesses published books in Santali, but these books use the Roman script as the native “Ol Chiki” script is not taught. We encountered only one individual in the entire village who could write fluently in Santali, but she also used Roman script instead of Santali “Ol Chiki” script. This highlights that there is a severe gap in Santali written language transmission in this region. They also showed us a dictionary of the Santali language, which is written in Roman and Bangla script, and there are also poetry books of the Santali language. They read out some poems for us to learn Santali. Those poems were written   for children so that they could learn Santali efficiently.

4. Religious Life and Community Cohesion


The community's primary religious identity is Christian, while some smaller groups may practice Hinduism and other religions.  In the Santali dictionary, we found some information about the ancient religion of Santals. One of the ancient Santal deities is named “abge bonga”. There are different names of the deities for each Santali tribe, and they are considered as protectors of that tribe. Only the head of the family knows this deity's name. This deity is worshipped in the month of “Ashadh” during the sowing season and in the month of “Agrahayana” during the harvesting season. Only men are allowed to be present during the worship ceremony. Women are not allowed to be present, and they are not given the name or identity of this deity. The head of the family secretly tells his eldest son the name of this deity before his death (abge bonga). Chicken and pigs were sacrificed by Santals to satisfy this deity. This religion is not seen as much in the community in modern times.


Christianity acts as a significant influence on social structure and learning in the Santal community of Gopalpur village. Children from the Santal community who are Christian mostly attend missionary schools run by churches, where they are taught prayers in English, Bangla, and sometimes Latin, which indicates a very structured and formal religious education.
 In these schools, they follow very strict rules, and children are punished if they forget their prayers or cannot perform well in exams. From one of these students, we found an example of such punishments, which is that children are punished for making sounds like a donkey, which is very different from punishments in our traditional schools. 
The community is connected to a wider religious structure known as "Dharmapradesh". A "Dharmapradesh" consists of all the churches and missionaries of that specific region. For example, "Rajshahi Dharmapradesh" consists of all churches and missionaries of Rajshahi. Students from the missionary schools are sent to different such establishments for further religious and spiritual learning. Religious celebrations, particularly Christmas, play a very crucial role in strengthening the bonds within the Santal community. On Christmas, no one in the village cooks in their own home. Instead, they cook a large meal together for every member of the community and then sit together to eat like one big family. This practice symbolises the unity of their community and shared identity, which goes beyond individual family units. On this happy occasion, they play music, and everyone dances together and enjoys the festivities. While we were talking to them, they invited us to join them next Christmas, which was very kind of them and proves how warm and welcoming they are as a community. 5. Economic and Leisure Activities


The economy of the Santal community is primarily based on agriculture, like crop cultivation and raising livestock. In their leisure time, the people of the village sit together in informal gatherings where people converse with each other and engage in their traditional Santal handicrafts. A significant activity is the weaving of mats from date leaves, known as "ranga pati," which is a variation of our traditional “Shitol Pati”. They call this “ranga pati” because the date leaves are stained with different colours before weaving them into a mat to make colourful 
patterns. This art form demonstrates a significant cultural tradition and serves as a medium for showcasing the aesthetic expression of the Santal community. These activities give them time to unwind from their hectic everyday life. Even if they don't weave mats, they can just all sit together, people from all generations and converse with each other, which strengthens the community further. They also celebrate their occasions together as a big family.

Limitations 


Though we can declare that our data collection and analysis process for Santali syntax proceeded relatively smooth, we still had to face some limitations. Only 12 speakers from the small village of Gopalpur were included in our sample. The potential observer effect suggests the researchers' presence may have affected natural speech. When elucidating grammatical concepts, we encountered significant translation difficulties. Our expertise in assembling different levels of syntactic data was restricted by time constraints. Language was a major obstacle, but we also had to deal with the issue of recruiting participants and striking a balance between male and female participants and participants of different ages, since the majority of the village's males had left to work and the younger generation had attended school or college.


Moreover, the time limitation was also responsible for the lack of sufficient data. It was difficult for us to collect data on such short notice. This made it difficult to collect data on such short notice and led to data scarcity for some of the topics we had set for our report. Further research can be conducted on these topics, as these are under-documented and indispensable to being cognizant of Santali syntax.






















































Directions for Subsequent Studies


While this study propounds an initial account of Santali interrogatives, copular construction, negation placements and interpersonal relationships encoded through free pronouns, multiple domains remain unfolded for advanced exploration. Subsequent research may unfold many cross-dialectal variations to determine whether the patterns are available in other regions of Santali-speaking areas across Bangladesh, India and Nepal. Interrogative constructions may proliferate to incorporate embedded, indirect and echo questions accompanying prosodic cues that outline information structure. The sociolinguistic dimensions of the usage of pronouns across age, gender, geographical boundaries, status, formality and Bangla contact should be included in the subsequent research to unravel the intriguing patterns of Santali free morphemes.


Future research may apply models like Role and Reference Grammar (RRG), minimalism,  and Lexical Functional Grammar (LFG) to examine theoretical assumptions. Researchers may align Santali within extensive typological databases ( e.g., WALS) to differentiate features like Word Order, Interrogative Strategies and negation types with Munda and non-Munda languages.


























Conclusion


This study provides a comprehensive understanding of Santali syntax, where our research team explored many variations across Santali syntax. Our findings were also compared with existing literature to identify the variations and unique syntactic patterns we found at the site. The syntactic analysis of the Gopalpur dialect disclosed a conservative core structure (SOV, Postpositional) that is simultaneously undergoing particular morphological innovations. Key findings incorporate the discovery of the fused negation particle [bo-iɲ], a feature unique to this region, and a robust Zero Copula hierarchy in attributive clauses and so forth. While the lexicon signifies evidence of heavy borrowing from Bangla (Code-Mixing), the functional grammar, including Case, Tense, and Topic marking, remains strictly Santali, demonstrating that the language's syntactic frame is resilient to contact-induced structural change. A structured map of negative markers in Santali was covered in our research. We also discovered the strict use of honorific markers in the Santali language. Ultimately, this study substantiates that while the surface lexicon of Rajshahi Santali is permeable to Bengali influence, the underlying syntactic skeleton—governed by SOV head-finality and agglutinative morphology -- remains remarkably resilient. The aim is to accentuate significant linguistic features of Santali, therefore, contributing to the collective conceptual foundations for potential areas warranting further examination.
